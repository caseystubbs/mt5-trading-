# Drop Momentum, Test Carry-Value Blends, Expect Little

A G8 FX system can have a small positive long-run expectation, but not the one we built, and not one that can make a $1,000 account meaningfully profitable. The published record leaves one durable source of return in developed currencies: a diversified blend of **carry** (interest-rate differential) and **value** (5-year real-exchange-rate reversal), optionally with a small trend sleeve. That blend earned about **3.4% a year at 3.7% volatility** in Russell's 1999–2024 backtest and kept a **post-publication Sharpe near 0.6** in AQR's century of data. Over the same period every single FX style has been close to zero or negative since 2010. Our weekly G8 momentum engine (−6.5%/yr before costs, Sharpe −0.24) is not a coding failure. It matches a literature that dates a structural break in G10 momentum to **August 2001** and measures its 2010–2020 out-of-sample Sharpe at **−0.68**. Our design (2 pairs, weekly, spot-only) then made a dead factor worse. Three frictions shrink whatever edge survives. Retail swap fees of 0.5–1.0% of notional per position per year take a third to half of the 1.6–3% gross premium a G10 style earns. Carry crashes arrive with no usable warning. And the 0.01-lot minimum forces a $1,000 account to run at **14–34% volatility** instead of the 4–6% the evidence assumes. My honest expectation for the best spec is a **gross Sharpe of 0.2–0.5 and net 0.1–0.35**. No demo forward test can confirm a number that small: a 0.3 Sharpe needs about 44 years of data to reach t = 2. The recommendation follows from that. Stop tuning momentum. Keep the demo for infrastructure. Fetch rates and value data once from outside the sandbox. Backtest a monthly G8 carry-plus-value blend against pass criteria written down before the run. Commit real money only at $10,000 or more, and treat the project as a research and teaching asset, not an income source.

## The momentum loss matches two decades of published failure

Run through the live engine code on Fed H.10 rates over 1,397 weekly rebalances, the MacroFX v1.1 signal lost **−6.5% a year before costs (Sharpe −0.24)** and −7.5% with weekday costs. Filled at the Sunday reopen it lost **−24.9% a year**, of which cost drag alone was −21.9% ([internal backtest](../backtest/REPORT.md)). Each horizon tested alone was flat or negative: 1-month −0.33, 3-month −0.05, 6-month −0.24, 12-month −0.02. The loss appeared in both 2000–2012 and 2013–2026.

Two details matter more than the headline. First, the loss is **not statistically strong (t = −1.26 before costs)**. Two random pairs held the same way scored a median Sharpe of −0.07, with a 95th percentile of +0.12 ([internal backtest](../backtest/REPORT.md)). The correct reading is "no edge", not "a reliable loser whose signal can be flipped". Second, the **−86% maximum drawdown** before costs reflects sizing as much as signal. Each position is held at 1× equity notional ([backtest script](../../scripts/backtest_macrofx.py)), so two concentrated crosses ran at roughly 27% annualised volatility (6.5 ÷ 0.24). That is about three times the 7–10% at which academic momentum portfolios run ([AMP 2013](https://w4.stern.nyu.edu/facdir/lpederse/papers/ValMomEverywhere.pdf); [Burnside, Eichenbaum & Rebelo](https://www.nber.org/papers/w16942.pdf)).

The published record predicted this result. Even in-sample, a 10-currency developed universe earned only about **3.0–3.5% a year from 12-1 momentum, at a Sharpe near 0.33 with t-statistics below 2**, over 1979–2011 ([AMP 2013](https://w4.stern.nyu.edu/facdir/lpederse/papers/ValMomEverywhere.pdf)). Menkhoff, Sarno, Schmeling and Schrimpf produced the famous 9.5%-a-year, 0.95-Sharpe figures from up to 48 currencies. Restricted to developed currencies, they found momentum returns **"much smaller and basically non-existent after transaction costs"**, with profits concentrated in high-volatility, high-country-risk currencies ([BIS WP 366](https://www.bis.org/publ/work366.pdf)).

The decisive out-of-sample test came from Hutchinson et al. (2022). In the ten most liquid currencies, cross-sectional momentum earned **−3.51% a year gross from February 2010 to April 2020, Sharpe −0.68 (t = −2.20)**. Across 123 momentum specifications the average out-of-sample Sharpe was −0.42, and the structural break in G11 momentum falls in **August 2001** ([Hutchinson et al. 2022](https://repository.adu.ac.ae/bitstreams/4912355c-88d1-434c-a489-e44e6ba6494b/download)). AQR's developed-market data agree: currency momentum's Sharpe fell from 0.40 in the original study samples to **0.08 after publication** ([AQR, "A Century of Evidence"](https://www.aqr.com:443/-/media/AQR/Documents/Journal-Articles/JOIM_How-Do-Factor-Premia-Vary-Over-Time.pdf?sc_lang=en)). Simple trend rules on major dollar pairs died even earlier. The Levich-Thomas filter portfolio's Sharpe went from 0.90 in 1976–1990 to −0.02 in 1991–2005 ([Neely, Weller & Ulrich](https://files.stlouisfed.org/files/htdocs/wp/2006/2006-046.pdf)). Our −0.24 sits between Hutchinson's full-sample (+0.18) and out-of-sample (−0.68) figures. A correct implementation of a decayed factor looks like this.

Four design choices then stripped out what little the academic portfolios kept. The first is **concentration**: a diversified 20-currency momentum portfolio earned Sharpe 0.62 over 1976–2010, against **0.43 for the average single trade** ([NBER w16942](https://www.nber.org/papers/w16942.pdf)), and holding the strongest-versus-weakest pairs out of 28 gives up that diversification. The second is **weekly rebalancing**, which trades the horizon with the least signal. One-week momentum across 63 currencies was insignificant (t = 0.85) and about zero in 2005–2013 ([Raza, Marshall & Visaltanachoti](https://mro.massey.ac.nz/bitstreams/8d7c87d0-7a19-46ab-b2ad-fa49a8176b48/download)), while weekly reversal appeared in mostly-G10 currency futures over 1982–2014 (t = 2.00) ([Della Corte & Kosowski](https://www.cicfconf.org/sites/default/files/paper_357.pdf)), which fits our 1-month horizon being the worst of the four. The third is the **return definition**. Academic studies use excess returns that include the interest differential, and Zhang finds that currency momentum is largely momentum in the carry and dollar factors ([Zhang, via IBKR Campus](https://ibkrcampus.com/campus/ibkr-quant-news/is-currency-momentum-factor-momentum/)); a spot-only, 28-pair strength meter in which the dollar is one of eight currencies nets out most of both. The fourth is the **horizon weighting**. Putting 55% of the weight on 6- and 12-month lookbacks leans on the weakest horizons even in Menkhoff's broad sample, where 12-month formation with a 12-month hold earned 1.89% a year against 9.46% for 1-month/1-month ([Menkhoff et al., JFE](https://faculty.washington.edu/ss1110/IF/Sarno%20Currency%20Momentum%20JFE%20%281%29.pdf)).

No momentum retune is worth funding, and Hutchinson's 123-variant average says specification choice will not rescue it. Flipping the signal into a reversal strategy is equally unjustified: our own loss is insignificant, and a reversal rule mined from the same data would need its own out-of-sample test. One cheap diagnostic is still worth running (spec 0 below): replicate Hutchinson's specification on the same H.10 file. If the engine shows positive returns before 2001 and negative ones after 2010, the code is fine and the effect is gone.

### Carry paid before 2008, then almost nothing until 2022

In-sample, G10 carry was the strongest FX style. An equal-weight G10 basket earned **3.96% a year at 5.06% volatility (Sharpe 0.78, skew −0.49)** over 1976–2013, and spread-weighted, risk-rebalanced versions reached Sharpe 1.02 ([Daniel, Hodrick & Lu](https://business.columbia.edu/sites/default/files-efs/pubfiles/6378/Daniel.Hodrick.Lu.Carry%20Trade.Critical%20Finance%20Review.2017.pdf)). An eight-developed-currency 3-long/3-short book earned Sharpe 0.75 over 1986–2006 ([Brunnermeier, Nagel & Pedersen](https://www.nber.org/papers/w14473.pdf)).

The real-money record after 2008 is bleak. The DB G10 Currency Future Harvest Index peaked at **315.27 on 25 July 2007 and set no new high through end-2022** ([DBV 10-K](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1354730/000095017023010813/dbv-20221231.htm)). Its annual returns from 2010 to 2019 swung between −7.63% and +10.39% and compounded to **+0.02% a year over the ten years to March 2020** ([SEC FWP 2020](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1354730/000119312520124811/d903986dfwp.htm)). The ETF that tracked it was liquidated in March 2023 because running it had become "unreasonable and imprudent" relative to its assets ([SEC 424B3](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1354730/000119312523013247/d407214d424b3.htm)). In May 2026 a Citi strategist said developed-market carry "hasn't made any money" since the crisis. He also noted that Citi's G10 basket was up about 4% year-to-date as rate gaps reopened, with Australian and Norwegian rates above 4% while Japan sat under 1% and Switzerland at 0% ([Reuters via MarketScreener](https://www.marketscreener.com/news/carry-on-trading-rate-based-g10-currency-bets-make-a-comeback-ce7f5bd2da8bf327)).

The sources conflict on how bad carry's decline has been. Hutchinson puts G11 carry's 2010–2020 Sharpe at −0.10. AQR reports carry's post-publication Sharpe at 0.51, above its original 0.33. The difference is mostly the window: AQR's post-publication period starts where each original study ended, so it likely includes the pre-2008 boom, while Hutchinson's starts in 2010. (The AQR original-versus-post-publication figures in this report were read from a table whose column mapping the research notes flagged for checking against the source.) Carry is regime-dependent. It needs rate dispersion, which vanished in the zero-rate decade and returned in 2022.

### Value is the most durable single style, and the slowest

Value has the best post-publication durability of any FX style. AQR measures a full-sample Sharpe of **0.60 across 20 developed currencies and a post-publication Sharpe of 0.69, against 0.72 originally** ([AQR](https://www.aqr.com:443/-/media/AQR/Documents/Journal-Articles/JOIM_How-Do-Factor-Premia-Vary-Over-Time.pdf?sc_lang=en)). Hutchinson's 2010–2020 value Sharpe was +0.30 but insignificant ([Hutchinson](https://repository.adu.ac.ae/bitstreams/4912355c-88d1-434c-a489-e44e6ba6494b/download)).

The signal that works is the 5-year *change* in the real exchange rate, not the PPP level. Barroso and Santa-Clara found the 5-year reversal added value while the real-exchange-rate level was "essentially irrelevant" ([CXO summary](https://www.cxoadvisory.com/strategic-allocation/optimized-currency-trading)). Menkhoff et al. found the raw 5-year signal earns a Sharpe of **0.44–0.51** in a 22-currency sample including emerging markets, with predictive power lasting one to two years ([Menkhoff et al. 2017](https://openaccess.city.ac.uk/id/eprint/14851/13/FXVALUE_Rev3_cepr.pdf)). Two cautions apply. Lagging inputs by two quarters to avoid look-ahead cut their raw linear Sharpe to about 0.31. And value has had a poor recent run: Russell's value sleeve returned −2.4% a year over the three years and −0.3% a year over the five years to March 2024 ([Russell](https://russellinvestments.com/-/media/files/emea/uk/institutional/insights/emea-arcs-research-paper-update-2024.pdf)). Value's main use is defensive. Its G10 skew is **+0.15 against carry's −0.90** ([Kroencke, Schindler & Schrimpf](https://repec.econ.au.dk/repec/creates/rp/11/rp11_10.pdf)), which makes it a natural hedge for carry crashes.

### Trend and dollar carry are diversifiers, not engines

Time-series trend held up better than cross-sectional momentum in-sample: currency forwards showed a 12-month-lookback t-statistic of 3.41 over 1985–2009 ([Moskowitz, Ooi & Pedersen](https://fairmodel.econ.yale.edu/ec439/jpde.pdf)). It then went flat. Hutchinson's G11 trend earned −0.99% a year (Sharpe −0.16) over 2010–2020. Multi-asset trend managed a Sharpe of only **0.05 over 2010–2018** ([AQR summary](https://theideafarm.com/alternative-investment/you-cant-always-trend-when-you-want/)). Industry reports described currency markets as "largely directionless" as recently as October 2025 ([Top Traders Unplugged](https://www.toptradersunplugged.com/?p=19314)).

Trend's value is crisis behaviour. In 2008 Deutsche Bank's momentum sleeve gained **+23.29% while its carry sleeve lost −23.35%** ([DBCR+ SEC filing](https://sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1159508/000095010310002326/dp18772_fwp-dbcrplus.htm)).

Dollar carry goes long all foreign currencies against USD when the average foreign rate exceeds the US rate, and short otherwise. It earned **5.60% a year at Sharpe 0.66** in developed currencies over 1983–2010 after bid-ask costs, with essentially zero correlation to cross-sectional carry ([LRV 2014](https://www.nber.org/papers/w16427.pdf)). Daniel, Hodrick and Lu split G10 carry into two parts. The dynamic-dollar component earned **5.54% at Sharpe 0.68 with skew of only −0.11**, while the dollar-neutral cross-section earned only **1.61% a year**, and its alpha fell to 0.10% once downside risk was controlled for. The crash risk sits in the part that pays least ([Daniel, Hodrick & Lu](https://business.columbia.edu/sites/default/files-efs/pubfiles/6378/Daniel.Hodrick.Lu.Carry%20Trade.Critical%20Finance%20Review.2017.pdf)). Our 28-pair strength meter nets out exactly the dollar component that carried the cleaner return. I found no post-2010 test of dollar carry, so its current strength is unknown.

### The blend works because the styles barely correlate

Carry, value and momentum/trend are nearly uncorrelated in every source: carry–momentum 0.07, carry–value −0.01 and momentum–value −0.09 in G10 over 1985–2009 ([KSS](https://repec.econ.au.dk/repec/creates/rp/11/rp11_10.pdf)), and carry–trend −0.06, carry–value −0.09 and trend–value −0.12 in Russell's 1999–2024 data ([Russell](https://russellinvestments.com/-/media/files/emea/uk/institutional/insights/emea-arcs-research-paper-update-2024.pdf)). Combining them cuts volatility and drawdown far more than return. Russell's carry/value/trend composite returned **3.4% a year at 3.7% volatility, with a −5.6% maximum drawdown**, against −28.7% for carry alone. Deutsche Bank's blended index stayed positive in both 2008 (+3.80%) and 2009 (+6.52%) while its carry and momentum sleeves swung ±23% in opposite directions ([DBCR+ SEC filing](https://sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1159508/000095010310002326/dp18772_fwp-dbcrplus.htm)). Jordà and Taylor showed that conditioning G10 carry on value and momentum turned a naive Sharpe of 0.59 with monthly skew of −1.9 into 1.27 with positive skew over 1987–2008 ([Jordà & Taylor](https://www.nber.org/papers/w15518.pdf)). That is in-sample, and the out-of-sample window was only 2004–2008.

The case against the blend is real. Bartram et al., known to me only through a secondary summary, find combined currency-factor profits net of costs **negative for more than a decade since about 2008** ([Klement on Investing](https://klementoninvesting.substack.com/p/central-banks-killed-currency-trading)). Barroso and Santa-Clara's optimised multi-signal portfolio "faded or disappeared after 2005" ([CXO summary](https://www.cxoadvisory.com/strategic-allocation/optimized-currency-trading)). Russell's figures are model-based, and its composite earned only **1.4% a year over the five years to March 2024**. Even the best-evidence design delivered low single digits after 2010.

## Decay, crash risk and retail costs consume most of what remains

### Published FX edges decayed, and the cause is disputed

Hutchinson et al. replicated the published FX factor studies across 252 specifications. The average Sharpe fell from **+0.39 in-sample to −0.32 out-of-sample**. They date structural breaks to August 2001 for momentum, September 2007 for value and June 2013 for carry, and blame arbitrage after publication ([Hutchinson et al.](https://repository.adu.ac.ae/bitstreams/4912355c-88d1-434c-a489-e44e6ba6494b/download)). Neely and Weller found **no statistically significant positive Sharpe for any developed-currency technical portfolio over 2000–2012**; the top performers were emerging-market carry ([Neely & Weller](https://files.stlouisfed.org/files/htdocs/wp/2011/2011-021.pdf)). Of 7,650 rules tested on six USD pairs over 1994–2014, none survived a data-snooping test, and the out-of-sample portfolio Sharpe was 0.027 ([Zarrabi, Snaith & Coakley](https://repository.essex.ac.uk/18805/1/1-s2.0-S1057521916301958-main.pdf)).

Professional managers lived the same decay. FX Concepts fell from a **$12 billion peak to $621 million** and closed in 2013 ([Euromoney](https://www.euromoney.com/?p=66955)), and the HFRI Macro Currency Index made roughly zero from end-2010 to early 2021 ([Verdad](https://verdadcap.com/archive/whatever-happened-to-fx)). Good years still happen, but episodically: the Barclay Currency Traders Index was up 5.57% through August 2024 ([The Full FX](https://thefullfx.com/?p=5244)).

The cause matters for anything launched now. Hutchinson blames arbitrage, which would make the decay permanent. AQR attributes weak trend to "muted market moves" and rejects crowding ([AQR summary](https://theideafarm.com/alternative-investment/you-cant-always-trend-when-you-want/)). Verdad points to converging rates and falling FX volatility ([Verdad](https://verdadcap.com/archive/whatever-happened-to-fx)), and Fan et al. trace carry's decline to the disappearance of the downside-risk premium ([Fan et al. 2022](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/intfin/v76y2022ics1042443121001670.html)). If the regime story is right, the 2022–2026 return of rate dispersion helps carry and value. If the arbitrage story is right, it does not. Both camps can be partly right, and nobody yet knows the split.

### Carry crashes arrive as sequences of losses

Carry's negative skew does not diversify away inside G10. It stayed near −0.7 whether the book held one pair or three on each side, and higher rate differentials predicted more negative skew ([Brunnermeier, Nagel & Pedersen](https://www.nber.org/papers/w14473.pdf)). Deutsche Bank's naive carry index lost about **10% in Q3 2008 and another 20% in October 2008**, erasing roughly 4.5 years of gains ([Jordà & Taylor](https://www.nber.org/papers/w15518.pdf)). A spread-weighted G10 book drew down about 35% that autumn, and hedges with fixed-delta puts barely helped ([Jurek](https://www.johnhcochrane.com/s/Jurek_currency_new.pdf)). Daniel, Hodrick and Lu show the large drawdowns come from runs of losses over 50–160 days, not single gaps ([CFR 2017](https://business.columbia.edu/sites/default/files-efs/pubfiles/6378/Daniel.Hodrick.Lu.Carry%20Trade.Critical%20Finance%20Review.2017.pdf)).

On our own H.10 data, **AUDJPY volatility went from 14.7% over 2000–2026 to 51.1% over September 2008 to March 2009** ([H.10 daily file](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/datasets/exchange-rates/main/data/daily.csv), computed in research). The live test came in August 2024. A carry book the BIS estimated at about **¥40 trillion ($250 billion)** was "hit hard" ([BIS Bulletin 90](https://www.bis.org/publ/bisbull90.htm)), and the yen rose about 5.6% against the dollar between 30 July and 5 August ([AMRO](https://amro-asia.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/12/20241219-Analytical_Note_Carry_Trade.pdf)).

The popular "exit when VIX spikes" rule lacks support. VIX moves coincide with carry losses but do not predict next week's (coefficient −0.03, insignificant), and rising VIX predicts *higher* carry returns three to four quarters later ([Brunnermeier, Nagel & Pedersen](https://www.nber.org/papers/w14473.pdf)). Timing carry with equity volatility turned its alpha negative (−1.28, insignificant), while scaling carry by its own realised variance produced **2.78% a year of alpha** ([Moreira & Muir](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w22208/w22208.pdf)).

### Swap fees take a third to half of a G10 premium

Retail carry arrives through daily rollover, and brokers add a fee to both sides of the tom-next rate: OANDA charges **1.00% a year on all G8 pairs** ([OANDA](https://www.oanda.com/us-en/trading/financing-fees/)) and IG charges 0.5–0.8% ([IG](https://www.ig.com/sg/help-and-support/cfds/fees-and-charges/why-is-overnight-funding-charged-and-how-is-it-calculated)). The fee reduces the credit on a long-carry position and enlarges the debit on a short one, so it is a fixed annual drag on every position: carry, value or trend. On current FRED 3-month rates the cost looks like this:

| Long pair | Gross differential | Kept at 0.5% fee | Kept at 1.0% fee |
|---|---|---|---|
| AUDCHF | 4.55% | 89% | 78% |
| USDCHF | 3.86% | 87% | 74% |
| USDJPY | 2.36% | 79% | 58% |
| NZDJPY | 1.52% | 67% | 34% |

Computed from [FRED IR3TIB01 series](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/IR3TIB01AUM156N) and the OANDA/IG fee levels; pairs with less than about a 1% differential pay swap in both directions.

Set against the premia, the fee is large. The dollar-neutral G10 carry cross-section earned 1.61% a year before costs, so **a 1% fee removes most of it**. Spreads are minor on weekdays and ruinous at the Sunday open. Our demo filled at **0.6–0.9 pips on weekdays and 13–36.5 pips at the Sunday reopen**. With eight micro lots fully turned over every week, that is about $37 a year (4% of $1,000) on weekdays against **$541–$1,518 at Sunday fills** (research computation from demo fills). The 5–6 p.m. New York rollover is the least liquid hour of the FX day ([CME](https://www.cmegroup.com/articles/2026/when-fx-futures-pause.html)). Monday execution, already built, fixes the largest single cost.

The base rate for retail FX is sobering. Between 74% and 89% of EU retail CFD accounts lose money ([ESMA](https://www.esma.europa.eu/sites/default/files/library/esma35-43-1135_notice_of_pi_decisions_on_cfds_and_binary_options.pdf)). Even at Interactive Brokers only **46.50% of retail forex accounts were profitable in Q3 2026** ([Interactive Brokers](https://www.interactivebrokers.com/en/index.php?f=3731)). A low-turnover weekly or monthly system avoids the overtrading and leverage that drive most of those losses ([Heimer & Simon](https://www.clevelandfed.org/-/media/project/clevelandfedtenant/clevelandfedsite/publications/working-papers/2015/wp-1522-facebook-finance-social-interaction-investing-pdf.pdf)). But I found no evidence that long-horizon systematic retail FX succeeds either.

## A $1,000 account cannot hold the portfolio the evidence describes

One 0.01 lot is **$561 (NZD base) to $1,323 (GBP base) of notional**. One standard deviation of annual P&L per micro lot runs from $33 to $96.5, or 3.3–9.7% of a $1,000 account, in a calm year. The smallest diversified book is one micro lot per position: four positions put about 4.6× gross notional on the account and produce **14–19% annual volatility in calm markets**, and eight positions put on about 9.2× and produce 19–34%. Both figures roughly double at long-run average volatility and quadruple in a 2008-type episode (research computations from the [H.10 daily file](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/datasets/exchange-rates/main/data/daily.csv)). The academic blends above run at 4–6% volatility, so a $1,000 account runs the same strategy at roughly three to six times its intended risk, with no way to scale down except by dropping positions.

| Equity | Micro lots per position at 10% vol target, 4 positions | Same, 8 correlated positions (ρ 0.3) | Granularity |
|---|---|---|---|

The table uses the trailing 12-month median pair volatility of 5.9%. Computed from the [H.10 daily file](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/datasets/exchange-rates/main/data/daily.csv).

A small account faces a trade-off with no good answer. It can hold one or two pairs at 5–10% volatility and give up the diversification that lifted momentum's Sharpe from 0.43 to 0.62 in Burnside's data. Or it can hold four or more pairs at 15–35% volatility.

My own illustration shows what that means. I simulated ten years of weekly returns, assuming normally distributed returns; that understates carry's fat left tail, so treat the results as optimistic. A strategy with a true Sharpe of 0.3 run at **18% volatility had a median 10-year maximum drawdown of about −39%** and a 26% chance of ending below its start. The same strategy at 5% volatility had a median drawdown of −12%. In dollars, a true Sharpe of 0.3 at 18% volatility is worth about 5% a year, or roughly **$50 a year on $1,000** in expectation.

The statistics also rule out learning much from a forward test. A t-statistic of 2 needs (2 ÷ Sharpe)² years of data: **about 44 years at Sharpe 0.3 and 16 years at Sharpe 0.5**. A demo run of months or even a few years verifies the plumbing. It says almost nothing about the edge.

Two things could ease the constraint. MidasFX may offer a cent or nano account, which would remove the granularity problem; this was not verified. Sizing by net currency exposure rather than pair count stops eight pairs from stacking into four to six times one currency, such as short JPY across AUDJPY, NZDJPY and CADJPY.

The same file also carries **Norway and Sweden through 9 October 2026**. Extending the universe to the full G10 used in the academic studies costs nothing on the data side, though NOK and SEK crosses will be wider at the broker. Menkhoff's two-quarter lag test shows how much a value signal shrinks once timing is honest.

## Five backtests to run next, ranked by evidence

The ranges below are my judgment. I took each source's long-run figure, discounted it for the post-2010 decay documented above, and discounted it again for a thin eight-currency cross-section, which is noisier than the 9–20-currency academic samples. "Net" deducts a 1% swap fee and realistic weekday spreads. Return and volatility figures assume risk sized as the evidence intends, not the forced 15–35% of a $1,000 account.

| Rank | Spec | Core rules | Evidence anchor | Expected gross Sharpe, 2000–26 | Expected net | Gross return / vol, properly sized | On $1,000? |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | Engine check | Hutchinson replication: G8 (+NOK, SEK) vs USD, 3-month formation, 1-month hold, top third minus bottom third, monthly | Sharpe 0.43 in-sample, −0.68 out-of-sample | Positive pre-2001, negative post-2010 | Diagnostic only | n/a | n/a |
| 1 | Carry + value blend, dollar-neutral | Equal-weight z-scores of lagged 3-month rate and of −Δ60-month real FX; long top 2 / short bottom 2 currencies; monthly | Russell 0.90 return/vol; AQR post-publication 0.63 | 0.2–0.5 | 0.1–0.35 | 1–3% at 4–6% | Only as 2–3 fixed micro lots |
| 2 | Dollar carry | Long the 7 foreign currencies vs USD if their average 3-month rate exceeds the US rate, else short; monthly | LRV 0.66 (1983–2010); DHL dynamic-dollar 0.68 | 0.2–0.5 | 0.1–0.4 | 2–4% at ~8% | Yes, via 2–3 USD pairs |
| 3 | Volatility-managed carry | G8 carry 2v2, exposure scaled by inverse realised variance of the carry book itself (not VIX) | Moreira–Muir alpha 2.78%; risk-rebalancing halves drawdown | 0.2–0.4 | 0.1–0.3 | 1–3% at 5–7% | On/off only |

| — | Do not test | Momentum retunes, signal flips, weekly rebalancing, rule mining | 123-variant average −0.42 | ≤ 0 | < 0 | — | — |

**Spec 0** should run first because it takes hours and validates the harness. Our H.10 file has the euro only from 1999 and carries no D-mark, so the pre-1999 leg needs a proxy (the Danish krone is in the file from 1971) or must run without the euro bloc.

**Spec 1** is the main candidate. Each month-end, score each currency on carry (its 3-month rate, lagged one month, net of the fee) and on value (the negative of its 5-year real-exchange-rate change, from the BIS REER once fetched, or from spot adjusted by the CPI differential as a stopgap). Average the two cross-sectional z-scores and go long the top two and short the bottom two currencies, mapped to two pairs (first versus eighth, second versus seventh). Rebalance on the first Monday after the London open, never at the Sunday reopen or between 16:45 and 18:30 ET. Keep the existing `select(..., buffer=...)` hysteresis and cap the net exposure to any one currency. Model swap as (r_base − r_quote − fee) per long, with a 1% fee as the base case and 0.5% as the optimistic case. Until the EA's spread log exists, stress crosses at twice the current 1-pip assumption. An optional third sleeve adds the spec 4 trend signal at one-third weight. Extending spec 1 to the full G10 is worth one extra run.

**Spec 2** is the simplest system the evidence supports: one decision a month about the direction of the dollar. On the latest values the rule is currently long USD. The average of the seven foreign 3-month rates is about 2.5% (AUD 4.51, NZD 2.98, CAD 2.28, JPY 1.46, CHF −0.05, Euribor 2.61, GBP 3.71 as of January), against 3.82% for USD ([FRED](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/IR3TIB01USM156N); [Euribor](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/datasets/euribor/main/data/euribor-3m-monthly.csv)). The risk is a single concentrated bet. In the zero-rate years the sign of the differential sat near zero and would have flipped on noise, so the test should include a dead band, for example no position when the gap is under 0.5%.

**Spec 3** doubles as an overlay on spec 1. At $1,000 it reduces to on/off: full size when the carry book's trailing one-month realised volatility is at or below its three-year median, one pair dropped above it, flat above twice the median.

**Spec 4** is a diversifier, not a standalone system.

Every spec should face the same pass criteria, written down before the run, and no parameter should be fitted to 2000–2026; use the literature defaults of 3-month rates and 60-month real changes. A spec passes only if it shows a gross Sharpe of at least 0.3 over 2000–2026, positive results in both 2000–2012 and 2013–2026 (the test v1.1 failed), a net Sharpe of at least 0.2 after the 1% fee and per-pair spreads, a Sharpe above the random-pair benchmark's 95th percentile of +0.12, and a maximum drawdown at fixed micro-lot sizing that Casey would actually sit through, on the order of 30%. Even a pass is not proof. A Sharpe of 0.3 over 26 years has a t-statistic of only about 1.5. The decision to go live would rest on the published evidence plus a consistent backtest, not on statistical certainty.

**Spec 0 passes decisively.** The same engine code that lost money after 2000 earned a significant +13.5% a year with a Sharpe of 0.75 from 1975 to 1999. The harness is right, and the edge disappeared on the timeline the literature describes.

**Value is the only single style that stayed positive in both halves**, at a Sharpe near 0.3, which matches the published post-2010 figures. Value plus trend clears the gross pass criteria: Sharpe ≥ 0.3, both halves positive, and above the random benchmark's +0.12. Its drawdown also roughly halves.

**It fails the net test.** A dollar-neutral book carries about 1× notional in pairs, so a 1% swap fee takes about 1% a year. That leaves roughly +0.5–0.9% a year, a net Sharpe of about 0.1–0.2, below the 0.2 bar. Swap credits and debits from the rate differential are not modelled either way. The 2013–2026 half is weak (Sharpe 0.13).

So the stopgap evidence supports the report's ranking: value is real but small, trend helps mainly by diversifying, and carry, which needs the rate data, is the missing piece that decides whether a blend clears costs.

## Bottom line for Casey: realistic as research, not as income

A G8 system with a small positive long-run expectation is realistic. A G8 system that makes a $1,000 account meaningfully profitable is not.

The best-evidence design, a monthly dollar-neutral carry-plus-value blend, has a defensible expectation of a net Sharpe around 0.1–0.35. Sized properly at $10,000–$25,000 and 5–8% volatility, that is roughly 0.5–3% a year: a diversifier, not an income engine. Forced into one-lot positions on $1,000, the same edge is worth about $50 a year in expectation, with a typical ten-year drawdown near 40% and a one-in-four chance of being behind after a decade. On top of that, retail swap fees will take a third to half of the gross premium, and the post-2010 record shows these premia can disappear for ten years at a time.

The momentum engine should be retired as a signal. It was not badly coded. The effect it targets has been gone from G10 currencies since around 2001.

The path I would take starts with keeping v1.1 on demo strictly as an infrastructure test of the bridge, the approval queue and Monday execution. In parallel, the EA should start logging daily swap rates and per-symbol spreads now, because MidasFX's real fee is the number that decides whether carry is viable at all. Next comes spec 0 to confirm the harness, then the one-time off-sandbox fetch of rates and REER data, then backtests of spec 1 and afterwards specs 2 and 3 against the pass criteria above. Real money should follow only if a spec passes, and only at $10,000 or more, or through a cent account that permits real sizing.

The negative momentum result, documented honestly with costs, random benchmarks and the literature that explains it, is the most credible thing this project has produced so far. It is worth more as a teaching asset than any retuned version of the signal would be as a trading one.

## Conclusion

The research changes the question. In developed-currency FX, no single signal has carried an edge since 2010. What survived is a property of the portfolio: several weak, nearly uncorrelated premia that cut each other's drawdowns when held together and sized to low volatility. That is exactly what a 0.01-lot, $1,000 account cannot hold. Account size and cost structure, not signal cleverness, are the binding constraints, and they apply to every spec on the list.

The timing cuts both ways. The 2022–2026 return of rate dispersion is the most favourable backdrop for carry and value since 2007, but August 2024 showed that the same backdrop rebuilds the crowded, leveraged positioning that produces crashes. A system launched now inherits both. What the backtest harness has proven is that it can kill a bad idea cheaply, before real money finds out the hard way. Holding the next idea to that same standard matters more than which idea comes next.
